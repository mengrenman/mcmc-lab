Bayesian inference

Turn data into a probability distribution over the thing you don't know. Start with a coin whose bias is unknown and compute the answer exactly. Then let MCMC find the same answer, the approach that still works when exact computation doesn't.

Prior × likelihood = posterior

Let \(p\) be the coin's probability of heads. The likelihood says how probable the observed tosses are if the bias is \(p\). For \(k\) heads in \(n\) tosses it is \(p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\), and its peak, \(k/n\), is the maximum-likelihood estimate. The prior \(P(p)\) is what you believed before tossing. Bayes' theorem combines the two:

\[P(p \mid \text{data}) = \frac{P(\text{data} \mid p)\,P(p)}{P(\text{data})}\]

\(P(\text{data})\) only normalizes the result, so the posterior is prior × likelihood, rescaled to integrate to 1.

Each toss updates the posterior. Updating after every toss gives exactly the same posterior as updating once with all the tosses, because the likelihoods multiply.

Try this

  1. Load 9 heads in 10 tosses. Maximum likelihood says \(p = 0.9\). Under the trusting prior (\(M = 100\)) the posterior's peak only moves from 0.5 to about 0.57. Under the flat prior it matches the likelihood.
  2. Switch to the suspicious prior. The posterior is exactly zero at \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) however much data you add, because a belief held with total certainty can never be revised.
  3. Clear the data, set the true bias to 0.6, and toss 10 at a time. The credible interval narrows roughly like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), and the prior matters less and less.

Posterior predictions

The posterior answers more than "what is \(p\)?". The probability of two heads in a row, for example, is the average of \(p^2\) over the posterior. Compare it with the same average over the prior (shown under the tile) to see how much the data changed your prediction.

Here everything is computed exactly on a fine grid of \(p\) values. That works for one unknown, but a grid with 100 points per axis needs \(100^d\) points in \(d\) dimensions. That is why the next lab samples the posterior instead.

Sampling the posterior with Metropolis

MCMC needs only the unnormalized posterior, prior × likelihood. \(P(\text{data})\) never has to be computed. Propose \(p' = p + \Delta p\) with \(\Delta p\) uniform in \([-c, c]\). A proposal outside \([0, 1]\) has zero prior probability, so it is rejected. Otherwise accept with probability

\[\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{P(\text{data} \mid p')\,P(p')}{P(\text{data} \mid p)\,P(p)}\right)\]

Strong prior, lots of data

In the book's example the prior insists the coin lands heads about 90% of the time, while the data say about 51.5%. With 1,000 tosses the data win, but the prior still shifts the answer. The posterior peaks near 0.53, not at the maximum-likelihood 0.515. Hover over the charts to compare.

Two unknowns: the mean and width of a Gaussian

Now the data are \(n\) numbers drawn from a Gaussian whose mean \(\mu\) and width \(\sigma\) are both unknown. With flat priors on \(\mu\) and on \(\sigma > 0\), the posterior is \(e^{-S(\mu, \sigma)}\), where (book eq. 6.10)

\[S = n\left[\frac{(\mu - \bar{x})^2 + s^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\sigma\right]\]

Here \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean and \(s^2 = \overline{x^2} - \bar{x}^2\). Only \(n\), \(\bar{x}\) and \(s\) enter, so they carry everything the data say about \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). Metropolis samples the pair directly. The book also shows HMC and Gibbs versions.

Where the exact answers come from

With flat priors this posterior can be integrated by hand. \(\mu\) follows a Student-t distribution with standard deviation \(s/\sqrt{n-4}\). \(\sigma\) has mean \(\sqrt{n s^2/2}\;\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-3}{2}\right)/\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-2}{2}\right)\). The chain knows none of this. It only evaluates \(S\), and the sampler checks confirm it recovers these values.

Flat priors are one choice. A \(1/\sigma\) prior is another common one, and would shift the \(\sigma\) posterior slightly toward smaller values.
