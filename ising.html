The Ising model

A grid of magnetic spins, each pointing up or down, that suddenly orders itself as the temperature drops. It's the classic test bed for MCMC, and a place where a clever algorithm beats a simple one by orders of magnitude.

A lattice of spins

Each site carries a spin \(s_i = \pm 1\), and neighboring spins prefer to align:

\[E = -\sum_{\langle ij \rangle} s_i s_j, \qquad P(\text{configuration}) \propto e^{-E/T}\]

The sum runs over nearest-neighbor pairs on a periodic \(L \times L\) grid. Below the critical temperature \(T_c = 2/\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \approx 2.269\), most spins agree and the lattice is magnetized. Above it, they are disordered.

Metropolis proposes flipping one spin and accepts with probability \(\min(1, e^{-\Delta E/T})\). Heat bath redraws each spin from its exact distribution given its four neighbors. Wolff grows a cluster of aligned spins, adding each aligned neighbor with probability \(1 - e^{-2/T}\), then flips the whole cluster at once. That move is always accepted.

A Wolff "sweep" is a fixed number of cluster flips, chosen so that about \(N = L^2\) spins flip on average. That way every algorithm does about the same work per sweep. Changing \(T\) or the algorithm keeps the lattice and restarts the averages.

Why Wolff needs no accept/reject step

The bond probability \(1 - e^{-2/T}\) is tuned so that the energy cost of flipping the cluster is exactly canceled by the probability of building that same cluster forward versus backward. Detailed balance then holds with an acceptance probability of 1. The details are in book §6.2.5.

The exact values quoted in the tiles are for an infinite lattice (Onsager's solution). A finite lattice is slightly off, most visibly near \(T_c\).

Critical slowing down

Near \(T_c\), aligned regions of every size appear. A single-spin algorithm has to wear them down one spin at a time, so its autocorrelation time grows steeply. For Metropolis at \(T_c\) it grows roughly like \(L^{2.2}\). Wolff flips whole regions at once and barely slows down. This scan sweeps through temperatures and measures \(\langle |m| \rangle\), \(\langle e \rangle\) and \(\tau_{\text{int}}\) of \(|m|\) at each one.

Each algorithm keeps its latest scan, so you can run all three and compare them.

    Try this

    1. Run the scan with Metropolis, then with Wolff. Compare the \(\tau_{\text{int}}\) peaks at \(T_c\).
    2. Rerun Metropolis with \(L = 48\). The peak grows. With \(L = 16\) it shrinks. That size dependence is critical slowing down.
    3. Look at \(\langle |m| \rangle\) above \(T_c\). On a finite lattice it doesn't reach zero, because \(|m|\) of pure noise is about \(1/L\). The gray Onsager curve is for an infinite lattice.

    Reading the \(\tau_{\text{int}}\) chart fairly

    All three algorithms are measured in sweeps of equal work: \(N\) single-spin updates, or a fixed number of Wolff clusters that flip \(N\) spins on average. Without that, Wolff would look better or worse just from how its "step" is defined.

    Why a fixed number of clusters? Stopping as soon as \(N\) spins have flipped sounds equivalent, but it biases the measurements. The cluster that crosses the line tends to be a large one (the inspection paradox), and large clusters go with particular states of the lattice. The sampler checks include a test that catches this.

    At low \(T\), heat bath and Metropolis look fast only because \(|m|\) barely moves. The slow mode there, flipping the whole lattice from up to down, never appears in \(|m|\).
