Lattice gauge theory

Lattice QCD computes the masses of protons and pions from first principles by sampling quark and gluon fields with MCMC on supercomputers. The real thing is four-dimensional, with 3 × 3 matrices on every link. This page runs the standard two-dimensional toy model that lattice-QCD algorithms are developed and tested on. It has the same ingredients (fields on links, confinement, topology, and quarks handled by pseudofermions, HMC and conjugate gradient) at a size a browser can handle.

Gauge fields live on links

Quarks sit on the sites of the lattice, and the gluon field sits on the links between them (book §6.4.1). In this toy, each link carries a phase U = eiθ, the gauge group U(1). Going around one square, a plaquette, collects the angle θP = θ1 + θ2 − θ3 − θ4, which is the lattice version of the field strength. The Wilson action is

S = β ΣP (1 − cos θP)

and configurations are sampled with weight e−S. Shifting every link by α(x) − α(x + μ̂), a gauge transformation, leaves every plaquette unchanged. Only gauge-invariant quantities like plaquettes and closed loops are physical. Large β means a fine lattice spacing, which is the continuum limit.

Changing β or the algorithm keeps the configuration and restarts the averages. Every measurement is compared with the exact answer for this finite lattice, from the character expansion.

Topological freezing

Changing Q means creating or destroying a whole unit of flux, which requires passing through configurations with a very unlikely plaquette. As the lattice gets finer, those configurations become exponentially rare, so any local algorithm stops moving between sectors. Real lattice QCD has the same problem near the continuum limit. It is critical slowing down again, like the Ising model at Tc, and an active research topic.

It also biases measurements. On a finite torus, charge Q spreads a flux of 2πQ over all the plaquettes, lowering each one slightly and adding a phase to every Wilson loop. At β = 6 on 16 × 16, ⟨cos θP⟩ is about 0.9128 at Q = 0 but 0.9115 at Q = ±2. A chain frozen in one sector measures that sector's value, with an error bar that knows nothing about the missing ones.

Adding quarks: HMC with pseudofermions

Quark fields are Grassmann numbers, which a computer cannot sample directly. They enter the action quadratically through the Dirac operator D, so they can be integrated out, leaving the weight det(D D†) e−SG for two quark flavors (book eq. 6.62). That determinant is far too expensive to compute, so the book rewrites it with a pseudofermion F:

det(DD†) ∝ ∫ dF exp(−F†(DD†)−1F)

Each trajectory then follows the book's recipe. Draw Φ from e−Φ†Φ and set F = DΦ. Then move the links with HMC. Every force evaluation needs χ = (DD†)−1F, which is found by the conjugate-gradient method (book Appendix E). This is the Schwinger model, with two flavors of Wilson quarks and the gauge field from above.

Trajectories have length 1, so ε = 1/steps. Any change restarts the averages. Turning quarks off gives the quenched theory, simulated with the same HMC but no determinant.

  3. Lower the mass from 2 to 0.02. CG needs more iterations, about 30 at m = 2 and 75 at m = 0.2 on 8 × 8. Light quarks make D nearly singular, and in real QCD at physical quark masses this cost dominates everything.

Checking the quark algorithm

On a 3 × 3 lattice the sampler checks run a completely different algorithm: Metropolis with the determinant computed exactly by LU decomposition. The two agree on the plaquette, and both differ from the quenched value by many error bars, so the check is sensitive to the quarks. The checks also confirm that the quark force matches finite differences of the action, that D† = γ5Dγ5, and that ΔH shrinks like ε².

From the toy to real lattice QCD

What scales up

  • Dimensions: 4 instead of 2, on lattices such as 64³ × 128 sites.
  • Links: 3 × 3 complex SU(3) matrices (8 real parameters) instead of one angle.
  • Quarks: 4 spin × 3 color components per site. The Dirac operator is a sparse matrix with hundreds of millions of rows.
  • Hardware: months on supercomputers, with most of the time spent in the same CG solve you timed above.

The result is worth it: the book's Fig. 6.25 shows hadron masses from lattice QCD agreeing with experiment.

Algorithms from §6.4 not run here

  • RHMC: for fractional powers of the determinant, such as a single strange quark, using rational approximations and a multi-mass CG solver.
  • Multi-time-step leapfrog: integrate the cheap gauge force with small steps and the expensive quark force with large ones.
  • The n-th root trick: split the determinant over n pseudofermions to make its force smaller and smoother.

The same toolkit simulates supersymmetric gauge theories, which through holography describe black holes in string theory. That is the research that led the authors to MCMC in the first place.
