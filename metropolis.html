The Metropolis algorithm

A random walk that proposes a small move, then keeps it or stays put depending on how probable the new point is. Watch one chain explore a distribution, then learn to tell a trustworthy run from a misleading one.

One proposal at a time

From the current point \(x\), propose \(x' = x + \Delta x\), with \(\Delta x\) drawn uniformly from \([-c, c]\). Then accept the move with probability

\[P_{\text{accept}} = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p(x')}{p(x)}\right)\]

Uphill moves are always taken. Downhill moves are taken only sometimes. If the move is rejected, the chain stays at \(x\) and records \(x\) again. Only the ratio of probabilities matters, so \(p\) never has to be normalized.

Changing the target, step size or starting point starts a fresh chain, so every statistic describes a single setting.

  5. Set \(x_0 = 12\) and watch the trace fall toward the typical region. Raise the burn-in until the shaded region covers that approach.
  6. Choose Two separated modes with \(c = 1\). The chain gets stuck in one bump, and the histogram looks confidently wrong. Try \(c = 6\).

What a good step size looks like

A step that is too small wastes time on tiny moves. A step that is too large wastes time on rejections. Between the two, the autocorrelation time \(\tau_{\text{int}}\) reaches a minimum. For the \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) target that happens around \(c \approx 3.5\), where roughly 44% of proposals are accepted and \(\tau_{\text{int}} \approx 3.7\). At \(c = 1\) the acceptance rate is 80% but \(\tau_{\text{int}} \approx 16\). Target acceptance rates are only rules of thumb. The number to minimize is \(\tau_{\text{int}}\).

With the Gamma target, any proposal with \(x' \le 0\) has \(p(x') = 0\) and is always rejected. Boundaries need no special handling.

Reading the diagnostics

Consecutive samples of a Markov chain are correlated, since each one starts from the one before. That changes how much information \(N\) samples carry.

Burn-in (thermalization)

The chain forgets its starting point only gradually. Samples taken before it reaches the typical region come from the wrong distribution, so they are discarded. The trace tells you how long that takes. Be generous, because discarding too much only costs a few samples.

Integrated autocorrelation time

\(\tau_{\text{int}} = 1 + 2\sum_{t \ge 1} \rho(t)\) is roughly the number of steps per independent sample. The sum is cut off at a window \(W\) where the remaining terms are mostly noise. Here \(W\) is the first lag with \(W \ge 5\,\tau_{\text{int}}\).

The effective sample size is \(N/\tau_{\text{int}}\). The error on the mean is \(\sigma\sqrt{\tau_{\text{int}}/N}\), not the naive \(\sigma/\sqrt{N}\).

Jackknife with binning

Split the \(N\) kept samples into bins of size \(w\). Each bin average is much less correlated with its neighbors once \(w \gg \tau_{\text{int}}\). Then recompute the mean with one bin left out at a time. The spread of these leave-one-out means gives the error.

At \(w = 1\) you get the naive error. The curve rises and levels off at the honest error, a check that does not depend on how the ACF sum was cut off.

Common mistakes (book §4.7)

  • Tuning the step size mid-run based on the chain's own history. Tune in a trial run, then fix \(c\) and start a fresh chain. That's why this lab restarts when you move the slider.
  • Pooling samples from runs with different settings and treating them as a single chain.
  • Poor random numbers. This app uses a seeded xoshiro128** generator, so any run can be reproduced exactly.
