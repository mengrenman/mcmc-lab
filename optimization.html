Annealing and replica exchange

MCMC can also find minima. Treat the function to minimize as an energy, sample e−f/T, and lower the temperature. This module shows where that simple idea gets stuck, how replica exchange gets it unstuck, and then puts both to work on the traveling salesman problem.

Stuck in the wrong valley

The book's test function f(x) = (x − 1)²((x + 1)² + 0.01) has its global minimum at x = 1. It has a second valley near x = −1 that is only 0.04 higher, and a hill of height about 1 between them.

Simulated annealing runs Metropolis on e−f(x)/T while lowering T. At high T walkers hop over the hill freely. As T → 0, e−f/T concentrates on the global minimum. The catch is that the hopping must stay possible while T is still small enough to tell the valleys apart.

400 walkers start at x = 0, on top of the hill. Each makes Metropolis moves with step size 0.1, as in the book. Changing a setting starts over.

Try this

  1. Run the default annealing. At first the walkers track the gray equilibrium curve. Then they fall behind: as the temperature drops they stop crossing the hill and freeze near a 50/50 split, while equilibrium climbs toward 100%.
  2. Make the annealing 10 times longer. It barely helps. Even over 300,000 steps about half the walkers end in the wrong valley, no better than an instant quench.
  3. Choose a fixed temperature of 0.3. The walkers do cross, but at that temperature the two valleys are almost equally likely (53% versus 47%), so there is nothing to find.

Why this landscape defeats annealing

Crossing the hill takes about e1/T attempts, so crossings stop once T falls to around 0.1–0.2. But the valleys differ by only 0.04, so e−f/T favors the global one strongly only below T ≈ 0.02. There is no temperature where walkers can both cross and prefer the right side. Physicists meet the same situation at first-order phase transitions (book §6.3).

Replica exchange: let the temperatures trade

Run M copies (replicas) at once, one per temperature T1 > T2 > … > TM, each with ordinary Metropolis. Every step, also offer to swap the configurations on neighboring rungs, accepting with probability min(1, e−ΔS), where

ΔS = (1/Tm+1 − 1/Tm)(f(Xm) − f(Xm+1))

The swaps leave every rung's distribution e−f/Tm unchanged, but they let a configuration climb to a hot rung, cross the hill there, and come back down. Each rung then samples its temperature correctly, including the cold ones that plain Metropolis cannot handle.

Temperatures run geometrically from 2 down to 0.001. Every replica starts in the wrong valley at x = −1. Step sizes scale like √T, since cold replicas only need small moves. Changing a setting starts over.

Try this

  1. Run with swaps on. Watch the orange walker ride up to the hot rungs, cross x = 0 up there, and settle into x = +1 on the way down. The measured rung (T ≈ 0.009) converges to its exact 98.8%.
  2. Reset and turn swaps off. Every rung is now plain Metropolis. The cold rungs never leave x = −1.
  3. Use only 8 replicas. The temperature gaps get wider, fewer swaps are accepted, and walkers travel the ladder more slowly.

Why the swaps are allowed

Treat all M replicas as one big system with weight Πm e−f(Xm)/Tm. A swap is just another Metropolis proposal for that system, and ΔS is its change in action. So detailed balance holds, and each replica, looked at on its own, still follows its own temperature (book §6.3.3).

The price is M simulations instead of one, but they are independent between swaps, so they parallelize naturally.

The traveling salesman

Visit N cities once each and return home, by the shortest route. There are (N − 1)!/2 distinct routes, which is already 181,440 for 10 cities and far too many to list for 30. Treat the route length rtotal as the energy and run replica exchange over routes. Each replica proposes a small change to its route and accepts it with probability min(1, e−β Δr). Neighboring replicas then offer to swap routes as above.

β is spaced evenly from βmax/M up to βmax, as in the book (which uses 200 replicas and βmax = 100). Every replica starts on the route 0 → 1 → 2 → … and back. For up to 10 cities, the true shortest route is found by checking every ordering.

Try this

  1. With 10 cities, run and compare with the brute-force optimum. Replica exchange usually finds it within about a hundred steps. Greedy descent stopped short of it for 16 of 20 random city sets in testing.
  2. Move to 60 cities with the book's swap move, then reset with the 2-opt move. A 2-opt move can uncross two edges at once. After 20,000 steps its routes are about 20% shorter, and even greedy descent with 2-opt beats replica exchange with swaps. The choice of move matters as much as the algorithm.
  3. Look at a long run's best route: it has no crossings. Any crossing can be removed by a 2-opt move that shortens the route, so a crossing always means the route is not optimal.

What "solved" means here

For more than about 10 cities nobody checks every route, so there is no certificate that the best route found is the shortest. Runs with different random seeds that agree give confidence, not proof. The book makes the same point about simulated annealing. For the book's 100-city example, replica exchange needed tens of millions of steps to settle.
